# Training Before Displacement: An AI Workforce Transition Pilot for Australia

**To:** Australian Government Department of Employment and Workplace Relations (DEWR)  
**Policy context:** Implementation of the Australian Government's March 2026 response to the *Future of Work: Inquiry into the Digital Transformation of Workplaces*, particularly its commitment to retraining and restructuring support for workers affected by AI.

## Executive summary

DEWR should establish a two-year, **$115 million AI Workforce Transition Pilot** to help 20,000 workers train and move into redesigned roles before displacement occurs. The proposal implements the Australian Government's commitment to support workers affected by workplace digitalisation. Australian evidence does not show broad AI-driven job loss, but DEWR has identified tentative slowing in highly exposed occupations. A preventative response is therefore more proportionate than economy-wide income support. The pilot would fund up to $5,000 per worker for accredited training and paid training time, require a 25 per cent employer contribution, and reserve access for small and medium enterprises (SMEs) and priority workers. It would combine employer knowledge of changing tasks with worker consultation and independent evaluation. Compared with individual training accounts or regulation alone, this option connects training to genuine jobs while sharing costs. Expansion should occur only if the pilot improves retention, redeployment and wage outcomes.

## Problem definition and current policy context

The March 2026 government response to the *Future of Work* inquiry commits to timely support, retraining and restructuring for workers affected by AI. It also places workers at the centre of the transition and identifies DEWR as the lead agency coordinating the response (Australian Government 2026). The National Skills Agreement provides an existing Commonwealth–state delivery structure, while its 2025–26 update makes AI-related VET capacity a focus (DEWR 2025).

The economic problem concerns tasks rather than instant elimination of whole occupations. Automation transfers tasks from labour to capital, creating a **displacement effect** that can reduce labour demand and the labour share. Productivity gains can increase output and demand elsewhere, while new human tasks produce a **reinstatement effect** (Acemoglu and Restrepo 2019). Jobs and Skills Australia (JSA) estimates that generative AI could augment around 55 per cent of tasks but automate about 13 per cent, meaning job redesign is currently more likely than wholesale replacement (JSA 2025).

Markets may still adjust poorly. Workers cannot easily finance training or know which skills employers will need. Employers may underinvest when trained workers can leave, while SMEs face tighter cash and coordination constraints. Basic AI awareness courses do not cover the wage cost of substantial retraining or guarantee redeployment. A targeted, co-funded pilot addresses these barriers without assuming mass unemployment.

## Analysis of workforce impacts

### Employment and tasks

DEWR found no broad labour-market upheaval as at February 2026. However, employment in the most AI-exposed fifth of occupations grew by 5.6 per cent after November 2022, compared with 9.5 per cent in the least-exposed fifth. Its model associates exposure one standard deviation above average with employment around 2 per cent below its pre-ChatGPT trend. DEWR stresses that these results are sensitive to modelling choices and do not prove AI caused job losses (DEWR 2026). This uncertainty supports a trial with monitoring, rather than a permanent universal program.

AI can complement workers as well as substitute for them. In a study of 5,179 customer-support agents, AI assistance increased productivity by 15 per cent on average, with larger benefits for novice and lower-skilled workers (Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond 2025). This suggests training can help workers capture productivity gains and may narrow experience gaps. Human work is likely to shift toward checking outputs, contextual judgment, communication and accountability.

### Wages, job quality and industrial structure

Higher productivity does not automatically produce higher wages. AI is owned as capital, so gains may flow to firms and technology owners unless workers gain new skills or bargaining power. OECD case studies find job reorganisation more common than displacement, but also report new skill demands and increased work intensity (Milanez 2023). Worker consultation can improve implementation by using employees' task-specific knowledge and identifying unsafe workloads.

AI may also change firm boundaries. Lower contracting and coordination costs can encourage outsourcing of routine cognitive services, while smaller internal teams may perform work previously requiring larger departments. These changes may weaken entry pathways if junior tasks disappear, even when total employment remains stable.

### Distributional effects

Exposure is concentrated in routine cognitive work such as clerical and administrative roles, while knowledge-intensive jobs are often more augmentable. Effects will differ across industries and cohorts. JSA warns that women, older workers, First Nations people and people with disability may experience the transition differently, including through unequal access to training or biased systems (JSA 2025). Casual workers, contractors, SMEs and workers outside major cities may also receive less employer-funded development. Eligibility and evaluation must therefore measure participation and outcomes across these groups, not only aggregate completions.

## Policy options and evaluation

The options are assessed against **efficiency** (whether support improves adjustment and productivity), **equity** (whether exposed and disadvantaged workers benefit), **feasibility** (delivery through existing institutions), and **Commonwealth fiscal cost**.

### Option 1: Individual AI transition accounts

Provide 25,000 workers in high-exposure occupations with accounts worth up to $4,000 for approved training. Including $10 million for administration, the two-year cost would be **$110 million**. Accounts increase worker choice and remain available when an employer will not invest. However, individuals may select generic courses that do not lead to redesigned jobs, while workers with less time or information may participate least.

### Option 2: Employer-supported training and redeployment pilot

Provide up to $5,000 each for 20,000 existing workers, covering accredited course fees and part of wages during training. Employers would contribute at least 25 per cent of training costs, consult workers, and submit a task-redesign and redeployment plan. The Commonwealth cost would be **$115 million**: $100 million in subsidies, $10 million for delivery and administration, and $5 million for independent evaluation. Germany's *Qualifizierungsgeld* provides a relevant precedent by replacing 60 per cent of net pay lost during qualifying training, or 67 per cent for workers with a child (Federal Employment Agency 2026). The Australian pilot is smaller and capped, limiting fiscal exposure.

### Option 3: Workplace AI consultation and review standard

Require employers to notify and consult workers before AI materially changes tasks, monitoring or employment decisions, and provide human review of consequential decisions. Allow **$15 million over two years** for DEWR and the Fair Work Ombudsman to develop guidance, support SMEs, monitor compliance and evaluate outcomes. This option improves accountability at relatively low public cost, and OECD evidence associates consultation with better worker outcomes (Milanez 2023). It does not, however, pay for skills development or the time required to train.

|---|---|---|---|---:|---|
| 1. Individual accounts | Medium | Medium–high | High | $110m | Flexible, but weak connection to jobs |
| 2. Employer-supported pilot | High | High with access safeguards | High | $115m | Best balance of adjustment and productivity |

## Recommended package and implementation

DEWR should implement **Option 2**, with worker consultation as a funding condition rather than immediately legislating all of Option 3. This package targets the current problem: changing tasks and uneven adjustment, not demonstrated mass unemployment.

| Timing | Action and responsibility |
|---|---|
| Months 0–3 | DEWR designs eligibility with JSA, Jobs and Skills Councils, states, unions, employer groups and TAFEs. JSA uses occupation exposure and labour-market data to identify priority roles. |
| Months 4–9 | Launch 5,000 places across clerical and administrative work and two additional high-exposure sectors. Reserve at least 40 per cent of places for SMEs and set participation targets for priority cohorts and regional workers. |
| Months 10–24 | Expand to 20,000 places if early completion and employer-quality thresholds are met. Registered providers deliver training; employers report task, retention and wage outcomes. |
| Months 18–24 | An independent evaluator compares participants with similar non-participants. DEWR publishes results and recommends expansion, redesign or closure. |

Key performance indicators should be: at least 80 per cent training completion; at least 75 per cent retained or redeployed with the same employer after 12 months; median real wages no lower 12 months after entry; improved worker-reported capability and job quality; SME and priority-cohort participation meeting reserved shares; and a measurable productivity improvement reported by participating firms. Publication of subgroup outcomes will show whether average success conceals unequal results.

## Risks and mitigations

**Deadweight cost:** firms may claim support for training they would have funded. Require evidence of material AI-related task change, a 25 per cent employer contribution and no pre-existing training commitment. **Low-quality training:** restrict funding to registered providers and courses mapped to a documented redesign plan. **Subsidised restructuring followed by redundancy:** require six-month retention or repayment, while allowing exceptions for genuine business failure. **Large-firm capture:** reserve 40 per cent of places for SMEs and monitor regional and cohort access. **Technology uncertainty:** use staged funding and update eligibility with DEWR's monitoring framework. **Work intensification:** require confidential worker feedback and consultation on workload, monitoring and job quality before the final employer payment.
