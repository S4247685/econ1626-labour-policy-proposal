# Training Before Displacement: An AI Workforce Transition Pilot for Australia

**To:** Australian Government Department of Employment and Workplace Relations (DEWR)  
**Policy context:** Implementation of the Australian Government's March 2026 response to the *Future of Work: Inquiry into the Digital Transformation of Workplaces*, particularly its commitment to retraining and restructuring support for workers affected by AI.

## Executive summary

DEWR should establish a two-year, **$115 million AI Workforce Transition Pilot** to help 20,000 workers train and enter redesigned roles before displacement. Australian evidence does not show broad AI-driven job loss, but DEWR has identified tentative slowing in highly exposed occupations. A preventative pilot is therefore more proportionate in this uncertain labour market than economy-wide income support. It would fund up to $5,000 per worker for accredited training and paid training time, require a 25 per cent employer contribution, and reserve access for small and medium enterprises (SMEs) and priority workers. Combining employer knowledge, worker consultation and independent evaluation would connect training to genuine jobs while sharing costs. Expansion should depend on improvements in retention, redeployment and wages.

## Problem definition and current policy context

The March 2026 government response to the *Future of Work* inquiry commits to retraining and restructuring support for workers affected by AI and identifies DEWR as the coordinating lead (Australian Government 2026). The National Skills Agreement provides an existing Commonwealth–state delivery structure, and its 2025–26 update prioritises AI-related VET capacity (DEWR 2025).

The economic problem concerns tasks rather than instant elimination of occupations. Automation creates a **displacement effect** by transferring tasks from labour to capital. Higher productivity can increase demand elsewhere, while new human tasks create a **reinstatement effect** (Acemoglu and Restrepo 2019). Jobs and Skills Australia (JSA) estimates that generative AI could augment around 55 per cent of tasks but automate about 13 per cent, making job redesign more likely than wholesale replacement (JSA 2025).

Adjustment may still fail because workers cannot easily finance training or predict employer needs. Employers may underinvest when trained workers can leave, while SMEs face tighter cash constraints. A targeted, co-funded pilot addresses these barriers without assuming mass unemployment.

## Analysis of workforce impacts

### Employment and tasks

DEWR found no broad labour-market upheaval by February 2026. However, employment in the most AI-exposed fifth of occupations grew by 5.6 per cent after November 2022, compared with 9.5 per cent in the least-exposed fifth. Its model associates above-average exposure with employment around 2 per cent below the pre-ChatGPT trend, but the results are sensitive and do not prove causation (DEWR 2026). This uncertainty supports a monitored trial rather than a permanent universal program.

AI can complement workers. Among 5,179 customer-support agents, AI assistance increased productivity by 15 per cent on average, with larger benefits for novice and lower-skilled workers (Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond 2025). Training may therefore narrow experience gaps as work shifts toward checking outputs, contextual judgment and accountability.

### Wages, job quality and industrial structure

Higher productivity does not guarantee higher wages because AI's returns may flow to firms and technology owners. OECD case studies find job reorganisation more common than displacement, but also report new skill demands and work intensity (Milanez 2023). Consultation uses employees' task knowledge and can identify unsafe workloads.

Lower coordination costs may encourage outsourcing and allow smaller teams to replace larger departments. Entry pathways could weaken if junior tasks disappear even while total employment remains stable.

### Distributional effects

Exposure is concentrated in routine cognitive work such as clerical and administrative roles, while knowledge-intensive jobs are often more augmentable. JSA warns that women, older workers, First Nations people and people with disability may face unequal access to training or biased systems (JSA 2025). Casual, regional and SME workers may also receive less development. Evaluation must report outcomes for these groups, not only total completions.

## Policy options and evaluation

Options are assessed for **efficiency**, **equity**, **feasibility** and **Commonwealth fiscal cost**.

### Option 1: Individual AI transition accounts

Provide 25,000 workers in high-exposure occupations with up to $4,000 for approved training. With $10 million for administration, the two-year cost is **$110 million**. Accounts increase choice when employers will not invest, but courses may not lead to redesigned jobs and disadvantaged workers may participate least.

### Option 2: Employer-supported training and redeployment pilot

Provide up to $5,000 each for 20,000 workers, covering accredited course fees and part of wages during training. Employers would contribute 25 per cent, consult workers and submit a redeployment plan. The **$115 million** cost comprises $100 million in subsidies, $10 million for administration and $5 million for evaluation. Germany's *Qualifizierungsgeld* provides a precedent by replacing 60 per cent of net pay lost during training, or 67 per cent for workers with a child (Federal Employment Agency n.d.).

### Option 3: Workplace AI consultation and review standard

Require consultation before AI materially changes tasks, monitoring or employment decisions, with human review of consequential decisions. Provide **$15 million over two years** for guidance, SME support and monitoring. Consultation is associated with better worker outcomes (Milanez 2023), but this option does not fund skills or training time.

| Option | Efficiency | Equity | Feasibility | Fiscal cost | Overall assessment |
|---|---|---|---|---:|---|
| 1. Individual accounts | Medium | Medium–high | High | $110m | Flexible, but weak connection to jobs |
| 2. Employer-supported pilot | High | High with access safeguards | High | $115m | Best balance of adjustment and productivity |
| 3. Consultation standard | Medium | High | Medium | $15m | Important protection, but insufficient alone |

## Recommended package and implementation

DEWR should implement **Option 2**, with consultation as a funding condition. This targets changing tasks and uneven adjustment, not unproven mass unemployment.

| Timing | Action and responsibility |
|---|---|
| Months 0–3 | DEWR designs eligibility with JSA, Jobs and Skills Councils, states, unions, employer groups and TAFEs. JSA uses occupation exposure and labour-market data to identify priority roles. |
| Months 4–9 | Launch 5,000 places across clerical and administrative work and two additional high-exposure sectors. Reserve at least 40 per cent of places for SMEs and set participation targets for priority cohorts and regional workers. |
| Months 10–24 | Expand to 20,000 places if early completion and employer-quality thresholds are met. Registered providers deliver training; employers report task, retention and wage outcomes. |
| Months 18–24 | An independent evaluator compares participants with similar non-participants. DEWR publishes results and recommends expansion, redesign or closure. |

KPIs are: 80 per cent training completion; 75 per cent retained or redeployed after 12 months; no reduction in median real wages; improved worker-reported capability and job quality; reserved SME and priority-cohort shares met; and reported productivity improvement. Subgroup publication will test whether averages conceal inequality.

## Risks and mitigations

**Deadweight cost:** require evidence of AI-related task change, employer co-payment and no existing training commitment. **Poor training:** use registered providers and documented redesign plans. **Redundancy after subsidy:** require six-month retention or repayment, except for genuine business failure. **Large-firm capture:** reserve 40 per cent of places for SMEs and track regional and cohort access. **Technology uncertainty:** stage funding and update eligibility. **Work intensification:** require confidential worker feedback before final payment.
